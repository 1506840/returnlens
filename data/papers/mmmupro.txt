MMMU-Pro: A More Robust Multi-discipline Multimodal Understanding Benchmark
Xiang Yue, Tianyu Zheng, Yuansheng Ni, et al. (MMMU Team)
CMU / Waterloo / OSU / IN.AI  —  arXiv:2409.02813v3 (ACL 2025 main)
Local extract for ResearchLens seed corpus.

Abstract
This paper introduces MMMU-Pro, a robust version of the Massive Multi-discipline Multimodal Understanding and Reasoning (MMMU) benchmark. MMMU-Pro rigorously assesses multimodal models' true understanding and reasoning capabilities through a three-step process based on MMMU: (1) filtering out questions answerable by text-only models, (2) augmenting candidate options, and (3) introducing a vision-only input setting where questions are embedded within images. This setting challenges AI to truly "see" and "read" simultaneously, testing a core human cognitive skill of seamlessly integrating visual and textual information. Results show that model performance is substantially lower on MMMU-Pro than on MMMU, ranging from 16.8% to 26.9% across models. We explore the impact of OCR prompts and Chain of Thought (CoT) reasoning, finding that OCR prompts have minimal effect while CoT generally improves performance.

1 Introduction (motivation)
GPT-4o reaches 69.1% on MMMU. Critical question raised: do current benchmark results truly reflect deep multifaceted understanding, or are models exploiting subtle shortcuts and statistical patterns without genuine comprehension? If models rely on superficial cues we risk overestimating capabilities and deploying systems that fail unpredictably in novel scenarios. Prior issues identified: text-only solvability of some questions, limited option space in multiple-choice formats, and the need to challenge joint modality understanding in a more integrated way.

2.1 Revisiting the MMMU Benchmark (the critique of the earlier benchmark)
MMMU consists of 11.5K college-level multimodal questions, 30 subjects, 183 subfields, 4-option MCQ, 30 image types. However, text-only LLMs can accurately answer some questions without any visual input. Two root causes:
(1) Text-Only Dependency: certain questions are relatively independent or irrelevant to the corresponding images.
(2) Shortcut Exploitation: even when questions require images for humans, models find shortcuts or correlations within the candidate options, leveraging pre-existing pre-training knowledge. Examples shown are answered correctly by Llama-3-70B-Instruct with no image at all.

2.2 Methods (three-step construction)
Step 1 Filtering: four strong open-source LLMs (Llama3-70B-Instruct, Qwen2-72B-Instruct, Yi-1.5-34B-Chat, Mixtral-8x22B-Instruct) answer MMMU without images; 10 repeats each, question counts as "answerable" if correct >5 times; exclude a question if >=3 of 4 models answer correctly in the majority of trials. Then randomly sample 1800 questions evenly across 30 subjects (60/subject).
Step 2 Augmenting options 4 -> 10, generated by GPT-4o, filtered by Claude 3.5, plus two rounds of human review; experts also removed 70 questions lacking clear image relevance/coherence -> 1730 questions.
Step 3 Vision-only input: annotators manually capture photos/screenshots of the question+options on a simulated display, varying backgrounds, font styles and sizes, so the model receives no separate text channel. Total 3460 questions = 1730 standard + 1730 vision.
Overall score = average of (standard w/ augmented options) and (vision-only).

3 Experiments (key numbers)
Random choice drops from 22.1 (MMMU val) to 12.8/12.4; frequent choice 26.8 -> 12.1. Human expert estimates 73.0-85.4.
GPT-4o (0513): MMMU val 69.1 -> MMMU-Pro standard(10opts) 54.0, vision 49.7 (overall 64.7 -> drops of 15.1 / 19.4).
Claude 3.5 Sonnet 68.3 -> 55.0 / 48.0. Gemini 1.5 Pro (0801) 65.8 -> 49.4 / 44.4.
LLaVA-OneVision-72B: 56.8 -> 38.0 / 24.0 (vision drop -32.8). VILA-1.5-40B: 51.9 -> 35.9 / 14.1 (-37.8). LLaVA-NeXT-72B: 49.9 -> 31.0 / 19.2.
Ranking changes materially (several models drop 1-5 ranks), showing the benchmark is not merely harder but differently ordered.

3.3 Impact of CoT prompting (contested finding)
CoT generally improved performance but benefits varied significantly: Claude 3.5 Sonnet standard 42.7 -> 55.0; GPT-4o Tech&Engineering +14.49, Business +14.66, Science +8.22. But LLaVA-OneVision-72B Art&Design -17.12, Humanities -4.46, Health&Med -1.56; VILA1.5-40B showed significant drops attributed to weak instruction-following and "boiled response format" problems. Conclusion: CoT helps structured reasoning tasks, is reduced or harmful in domains requiring subjective interpretation, and robust instruction-following is a prerequisite for effective CoT.

3.4 Does OCR help in the vision setting?
OCR accuracy measured via Levenshtein similarity: GPT-4o 92.3, Gemini 1.5 Pro 89.7, but LLaVA-NeXT-7B only 36.6. Adding an explicit OCR prompt ("first extract the question then solve it") changed accuracy negligibly for all models (e.g. GPT-4o 49.4 vs 49.7; InternVL2-40B 32.1 vs 28.9). Key negative result: high OCR accuracy does not translate into strong multimodal reasoning — LLaVA-OneVision-72B matches InternVL2-76B and GPT-4o mini in OCR accuracy yet lags far behind in MMMU-Pro Vision. Simple OCR is insufficient for the vision-only setting.

3.5 Qualitative analysis (failure modes)
Challenges with increased options: models select the closest answer rather than deriving a definitive one; conceptually similar options cause confusion. Increased cognitive load in vision-text integration: a case where the model perfectly extracted text from the image but still answered wrongly. Overemphasis on visual cues: chose League of Nations by fixating on a WWI image, missing WWII/UN context. Context switching: correctly defined objective function and constraints but misread the feasible region after switching between text and figure.

3.6 Error analysis (60 annotated GPT-4o errors, vision setting)
Same three broad categories as MMMU (perception / knowledge / reasoning) but reasoning errors rise to 46% vs 26% in original MMMU. Within perception errors, text recognition/OCR is NOT the primary bottleneck; the difficulty lies in integration and interpretation of visual and textual information.

3.7 Response length comparison
Classifying GPT-4o outputs into Descriptive vs Analytical sentences: under vision input, responses are significantly shorter and use more tokens for Descriptive rather than Analytical content — the cognitive workload of visual processing distracts the model from generating extensive reasoning chains.

4 Guide for future model training
Scaling LLM backbones consistently helps (GPT-4o > GPT-4o mini; OneVision-72B > 7B; InternVL2-76B > 8B). Controlled vision-encoder experiment on Cambrian (fixed data 1M + fixed Llama 3.1 8B): Siglip ViT-SO400M-14 scores MMMU 37.9 but MMMU-Pro Vision only 16.7, whereas self-supervised DINOv2 ViT-G-14 scores MMMU 37.1 but Vision 17.4 — language-supervised encoders look strong on MMMU yet collapse on MMMU-Pro Vision, suggesting future work should enhance visual feature learning while exploring integration of language-based and self-supervised objectives. Also: better cross-modal attention/fusion; CoT data generation targeted at domains where CoT is currently neutral or harmful; text-rich image generation pipelines to create reasoning-intensive training data.

5 Related Work positioning
Existing benchmarks fall short on expert-level domain knowledge and complex reasoning; "While MMMU made strides ... it still permits text-only models to find shortcuts."

6 Conclusion
Research directions: (1) models with consistent performance across settings, particularly bridging the standard vs vision-only gap; (2) enhanced vision-text integration for mixed-format inputs; (3) advanced reasoning techniques for higher question complexity.

=== Limitations (verbatim, dedicated section before references) ===
While MMMU-Pro improves upon existing benchmarks by filtering out text-only solvable questions and introducing a vision-only setting, some limitations remain. The dataset may still contain subtle statistical shortcuts that models can exploit, and its scope is limited to predefined disciplines and question formats. Additionally, while the vision-only input setting increases difficulty, it does not fully capture the complexities of human perception. Lastly, our reliance on approximated human performance rather than direct evaluation introduces potential biases in reporting accurate human expert performance.
